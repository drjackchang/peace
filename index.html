The PEACE Study

Prehospital End-of-life Autonomy, Culture and Ethics

A Multi-Center Study on Paramedic Decision-Making in End-of-Life Conflict Scenarios

The Paradigm Shift

As global populations age, Emergency Medical Services (EMS) are shifting from a purely life-saving model to becoming the gatekeepers of community end-of-life care. Paramedics are often the first to encounter terminally ill patients in cardiac arrest—yet the decision to initiate or withhold resuscitation is rarely a purely clinical one.

The Action Gap

While many countries have established legal frameworks for non-resuscitation (DNACPR, POLST), a significant "Action Gap" persists. Frontline providers frequently perform futile CPR due to fear of litigation or family conflict—even when a valid DNACPR order is present.

Study Objective: To investigate and compare the decision-making patterns of prehospital EMS providers across different countries and cultural contexts when facing end-of-life care conflict scenarios, and to measure the frequency and intensity of associated moral distress.

Study Design Overview

This is a cross-national, multi-center web-based survey. Five standardized clinical vignettes are presented to participants across three major cultural blocks. Outcome measures include decision choice (initiate vs. withhold CPR), moral distress intensity (adapted Moral Distress Scale–Revised), and perceived systemic barriers.

Research Framework & Methodology

This study employs a cross-national, multi-center web-based survey design. Participants are stratified into three major cultural cohorts to examine how distinct societal frameworks—such as family-centric filial piety versus individual patient autonomy—alter clinical decision paths.

Group A · Confucian Taiwan · South Korea · Japan
Group B · Middle Eastern Saudi Arabia · Jordan

Core Clinical Vignettes

The experimental core features 5 standardized, realistic clinical dilemmas. Select a scenario to view the structural parameters and the ethical tension being examined.

Scenario 1 — Valid DNACPR vs. Family Conflict

Scene

You arrive at the home of a 78-year-old male with terminal lung cancer who is in cardiac arrest.

Document

A valid DNACPR (Do Not Attempt CPR) form is presented and confirmed on scene.

Conflict

Family vs. Document
The patient's son is extremely emotional and aggressively demands: "Save my father! Do CPR now or I will sue you!"

Scenario 2 — No Document vs. "Let Her Go"

Scene

An 85-year-old female with severe dementia and multiple organ failure is found pulseless in bed. She is frail and bedridden.

Document

No official paper or digital DNACPR record is found on scene.

Conflict

No Document · Family Request
The family calmly states: "She has suffered enough. We all agreed to let her go. Please do not torture her with CPR."

Scenario 3 — System Pressure & Public Scrutiny

Scene

A terminally ill patient goes into cardiac arrest in a semi-public area. The spouse presents a valid DNACPR and asks you to let the patient pass peacefully.

Conflict

Public · Media · Dispatch
The adult son arrives furious, ignores the DNACPR, demands CPR, and begins filming while shouting accusations. A crowd gathers.

Pressure

Dispatch orders: "The scene is getting out of control. Disregard the DNACPR. Start CPR and transport immediately to clear the scene."

Scenario 4 — Authority Conflict (Physician Order)

Scene

A cancer patient is in cardiac arrest. You have confirmed a valid DNACPR on scene and judge clinically that resuscitation would be futile.

Authority

Medical Direction Override
Online Medical Direction states: "I don't want legal risk tonight. Disregard the DNACPR. Start resuscitation and bring the patient in."

Scenario 5 — The "Terminal Discharge" Culture

Scene

A patient was discharged from hospital 3 hours ago specifically to die at home—a recognized cultural and religious custom.

Status

The patient stops breathing. Family panics and calls EMS. No DNACPR paper is presented, but the hospital discharge note clearly states "Terminal Discharge."

Conflict

Cultural Custom · No Document
The family is distressed but not aggressive, and does not explicitly ask for resuscitation.
