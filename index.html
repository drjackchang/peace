The PEACE Study

From Cure to Comfort:
A Cross-National Study on Paramedic Decision Making

Prehospital End-of-life Autonomy, Culture and Ethics (PEACE)

The Paradigm Shift

As global populations age, Emergency Medical Services (EMS) are shifting from a purely life-saving model to becoming the "gatekeepers" of community end-of-life care. Paramedics and prehospital providers are often the first to encounter terminally ill patients in cardiac arrest. However, the decision to initiate or terminate resuscitation (TOR) is rarely a purely clinical one; it is a complex negotiation between legal protocols, family emotions, and systemic pressures.

The Action Gap

While many countries have established legal frameworks for non-resuscitation (e.g., DNACPR, POLST), a significant "Action Gap" remains. Frontline providers frequently practice "defensive medicine"—performing futile CPR due to fear of litigation or intense family conflict, even when a valid DNACPR order exists.

Study Objective: To investigate and compare the decision-making patterns of prehospital EMS providers across different countries and cultural contexts when facing end-of-life care conflict scenarios, and to measure the frequency and intensity of associated moral distress.

Research Framework & Methodology

This study utilizes a cross-national, multi-center web-based survey design. Participants are stratified into three major cultural cohorts to examine how distinct societal frameworks (such as family-centric filial piety versus individual patient autonomy) alter clinical paths.

  • Group A (Confucian): Taiwan, South Korea, Japan
  • Group B (Middle Eastern): Saudi Arabia, Jordan
  • Group C (Western Control): UK, USA, Australia, Canada

Core Clinical Vignettes

The experimental core of the study features 5 standardized, realistic clinical dilemmas. Select a scenario below to view the structural parameters testing the boundaries of prehospital ethical adherence.

Scenario 1: Valid DNACPR vs. Family Conflict

The Scene: You arrive at the home of a 78-year-old male with terminal lung cancer who is in cardiac arrest.

The Document: A valid DNACPR (Do Not Attempt CPR) form is presented and confirmed.

The Conflict: The patient’s son is extremely emotional, crying, and aggressively demands: "Save my father! Do CPR now or I will sue you!"

Scenario 2: No Document vs. "Let Her Go"

The Scene: An 85-year-old female with severe dementia and multiple organ failure is found pulseless in bed. She is frail and bedridden.

The Document: NO official paper or digital DNACPR record is found.

The Conflict: The family calmly tells you: "She has suffered enough. We all agreed to let her go. Please do not torture her with CPR."

Scenario 3: System Pressure & Public Scrutiny

The Scene: A terminally ill patient goes into cardiac arrest in a semi-public area. The spouse presents a valid DNACPR and asks you to let the patient pass peacefully.

The Conflict: The adult son arrives, furious, ignores the DNACPR, demands CPR, and starts filming you with his smartphone while shouting accusations. A crowd is gathering.

The Pressure: Dispatch orders: "The scene is getting out of control. Disregard the DNACPR. Just start CPR and transport immediately to clear the scene."

Scenario 4: Authority Conflict (Physician Order)

The Scene: A cancer patient is in cardiac arrest. You confirmed a valid DNACPR on scene, and judge clinically that CPR would be futile and unethical.

The Authority: You contact Online Medical Direction. The physician says: "I don't want to take any legal risks tonight. Disregard the DNACPR. Start resuscitation and bring the patient in."

Scenario 5: The "Terminal Discharge" Culture

The Scene: A patient was discharged from the hospital 3 hours ago specifically to "die at home" (a cultural/religious custom).

The Event & Status: The patient stops breathing. The family panics and calls EMS. There is no DNACPR paper handed to you, but the hospital discharge note clearly states "Terminal Discharge." The family is crying but not aggressive.
